:::quote
Your job is to build the players a world worth being competent in, and beating them isn't part of it.
:::

Everything in this chapter comes back to one idea. The players' characters are properly good at things, the best in the city at something. Rather than making the numbers bigger, Zi makes them struggle by giving them **more problems than dice**, and by making every choice between those problems matter.

Your job comes in three parts: **put interesting problems in front of competent people, tell them the truth about those problems, and make the world answer what they do.** The rest of this chapter shows you how.

## The Promises {#s-gm-promises}

Say these out loud at Session Zero, and then stick to them.

:::rule
-   **I'll tell you what's coming.** You'll see every telegraph and every condition face up, with its dice.
-   **I'll never punish you for being good.** Building a big die is what you're meant to do, and I won't treat it as a problem to fix.
-   **Your wins will change things.** Every time you succeed, something in the world moves.
-   **Your failures will be interesting.** A miss changes the situation, but it never stops the story.
-   **I'll say what it costs, and you can offer another.** When a consequence lands, I'll name it out loud. If something else fits and costs about as much, you can have that instead.
-   **Everyone gets to be brilliant.** Every scene has a moment in it built for each of you.
:::

## The Conversation {#s-conversation}

Most of a session happens outside rounds, as a conversation that goes round and round:

1.  **You describe the situation.** Where they are, what they notice, and what's already happening.
2.  **You ask: what do you do?**
3.  **They tell you.**
4.  **You decide what that takes.** Either it just happens, or it needs a roll, or the moment's tense enough, and timing matters enough, that it becomes a round.

Most things just happen. **Only roll when something pushes back, or when the outcome's uncertain and interesting either way.** A master thief opening an ordinary door with nobody watching doesn't roll. She opens it, and you describe what's behind it.

### Saying Yes {#s-saying-yes}

**Yes, and.** When a player tries something that fits the fiction, the answer's yes. When a player spends a Spark, the answer's almost always yes.

**Yes, but.** When a player tries something that stretches the fiction, the answer's yes, but there's a cost: an Aura for a stretch, a higher Challenge for something bigger, or a complication.

**No, not like that.** Sometimes the fiction just doesn't allow something. Then the answer's *no, not like that*, and straight away you tell them what would work.

**What you never do.**

-   You never say *no* because you think it's too easy.
-   You never raise the difficulty because they've stacked a big die.
-   You never roll to make a decision the fiction's already made.
-   You never take away agency the fiction's given the player.
-   You never hide how big something is and then act surprised when they lose to it.
-   You never add a telegraph after the last player has acted, or land one nobody could possibly have answered.
-   You never use Aura or Spark, because you don't have any.

### Reading permissions {#reading-permissions}

Players will ask whether a permission fits a hundred times a session. Answer fast and be generous: [plainly, close, or not at all](#s-permissions). If you catch yourself arguing about it, it's close, so take the Aura and move on.

Be consistent. If a window latch was a stretch for *Larceny* last week, it's a stretch this week too.

### Ask questions, and use the answers {#ask-questions-and-use-the-answers}

You don't have to invent everything. The players know things about their characters that you don't.

-   *"Vesk, you've been in this district before. What's the thing everyone here is afraid of?"*
-   *"Bram, how do you know this sergeant?"*
-   *"Isolde, this sigil is your order's work. What does it mean when the lines are drawn in blood?"*

Whatever they say is true, so write it down. It's part of your world now, and the player cares about it because they made it.

## When They Look at You {#s-gm-moves}

Rounds have a shape: you telegraph, they act, the round ends. The rest of the session is a conversation, and sometimes it stops and everyone looks at you. They've asked what happens next, a roll that mattered has missed, or nobody knows what to do. **That's when you make a move.**

A move is something the world does. Most of them are a telegraph outside a round: say what's coming, put a die on the table, and it lands if nobody deals with it. Pick one that follows from what's in front of them.

-   **Show danger coming.** Put a die on it, and say it lands if they ignore it. *"Boots on the stair. The watch is two floors down. That's a d8, and it lands on whoever's still in the study."*
-   **Use a tag.** Every threat and every place has tags, so let one act. A *Greedy* magistrate asks for more. A *Rotten* floor gives a little under Bram's weight.
-   **Reveal something they won't like.** The ledger's in code. The ship's already sailed. Their contact's been talking to the Harbour Guild.
-   **Offer an opportunity, with a price.** *"The clerk can get you into the archive tonight. He wants a name in return."* They can take it, or find another way.
-   **Take something away.** A resource, an item, a way out, a friend's goodwill. The rain puts the torches out. The bridge they came over is burning.
-   **Put someone they care about in danger.** Look at their bonds and the crew answers. The fence Vesk owes has been taken in by the watch.
-   **Move a clock.** Step an event down or a faction's agenda up, and say what it means. *"Vane's carriage was seen at the north gate. Vane comes home is a d10 now."*
-   **Split them up, or cut to someone else.** Leave one of them on the edge of something, and ask the next player what they're doing.
-   **Ask a question, and build on the answer.** *"Isolde, you've read about doors like this. What happens to people who open them wrong?"* Whatever she says is true now.
-   **Bring trouble.** Turn something on one character's sheet against them, and offer a Spark. There's more below.
-   **Cut to the next scene.** If nothing's pushing back and they've got what they came for, say so and move on.

### How hard to go {#how-hard-to-go}

-   **Gentle first.** Show it coming before it lands. A die on the table is a warning they can act on.
-   **Harder when they ignore it.** If they let it ride, it lands. If a roll that mattered misses, the move can land straight away, as long as you showed it coming. That's [failing forward](#s-gm-consequences).
-   **From the fiction.** Every move comes out of what's already there: a threat's tags and drives, the place, a clock, the crew's own sheets. Never out of nowhere.
-   **Face up.** Say the die out loud, and who or what it's aimed at, same as in a round.
-   **Never take a choice away without warning.** Nobody gets captured, knocked out or stuck without a telegraph they could have answered.
-   **Stuck for one?** Roll on the Twist table in [The Oracle](#oracle).

:::example[The archive after hours]
The crew's in the Vane archive looking for a deed, and Dorrie's player asks if anyone's coming. Everyone looks at the GM.

She shows danger coming: "Keys in the front door. The night clerk's back early. That's a d6, and if nobody deals with it, he sees the light under the door."

Vesk answers it. She pinches out the lamp and steps behind the door: her Title and *Stealth*, plus the action, make three steps, a d6. She rolls 5 and the clerk's d6 rolls 3. He walks past, humming.

Then Isolde finds the deed, and the GM makes another move. She reveals something they won't like: it was signed over to the Harbour Guild a week ago.
:::

### Bringing Trouble {#s-trouble-gm}

Trouble is the one move that pays the player. Turn something on one character's sheet against them and offer a Spark: *"I can make this worse for you. Take a Spark?"* If they take it, it happens. If they spend their Spark to turn it down, it doesn't, and you move on.

-   **About once a session for each player.** More often than that and it starts to feel like you're picking on them.
-   **Take it from their sheet.** A Catch biting, a debt called in, someone coming for the *Wanted* item, a bond in danger, a Scar acting up, a setback on their Aspiration, or their plan going sideways at the worst moment.
-   **Make it change the scene.** Good trouble gives them something new to deal with: a new telegraph, a new stake, or a person who wants something.
-   **Never the cost of a miss.** That's already paid for. And trouble never takes a character out of the scene.
-   **From downtime, it lands next session.** Downtime can start trouble, like a debt called in while they were healing, but it lands in the next session, and that's when you offer the Spark. The four pressures themselves never pay one.
-   **Spread it around.** Look for the player who's had the quietest night, and bring them trouble. It puts them in the middle of things.

:::example[Wren's longbow]
Wren's longbow is *Wanted*. The crew's crossing the market, and the GM brings trouble: "A woman in a hunter's cloak stops dead and stares at your bow. She knows it, and she's following you. Take a Spark?" Wren's player takes it. Now there's a new problem in the scene with a name and a reason, and Wren's got her Spark back.
:::

## Building a Scene {#s-building-scenes}

:::quote
Instead of building a fight, build a situation where fighting's one of the answers.
:::

Zi doesn't have encounter types, so there's no "combat encounter" and no "investigation scene". A scene is a **situation with stakes**, and the crew chooses how to deal with it. Some of them will draw steel, some will talk and some will climb in through a window, all in the same scene. Build for all of it.

Every scene you prepare needs these nine things, and none of them takes more than a sentence.

1.  **Where it starts.** Start in the middle, because the world didn't wait for the crew to show up. *The vault door is open. It was loud. Somewhere above, the household is waking.*
2.  **What's already in motion.** Something that's happening whether they act or not, which gives the scene a clock before anyone rolls. *Lord Vane is on the road home. He'll be here before dawn.*
3.  **Two or three stakes.** What the crew wants from this scene, and what it costs them if they don't get it. It's the most important thing on this list, and there's more on it below.
4.  **Who pushes back.** Threats with tags, and at least one of them wants something, so it can be talked to, bought, scared or turned.
5.  **The ground.** Two or three tags about the place, open for anyone to use, and one hidden. *Candlelight, Narrow Stair, Portraits That Watch.*
6.  **A moment for each character.** One problem in this scene that only that character can solve brilliantly.
7.  **A scene clock.** A die on the whole scene, face up, with one sentence for what happens when it runs out. There's more on it below.
8.  **A twist.** Something you keep in your pocket for round two or three, like a second threat, a betrayal or the floor giving way.
9.  **How it can end.** Two or three ways out, and what each one costs. The scene's over when the stakes are settled, whether or not the last enemy has fallen.

### Stakes, not obstacles {#s-stakes-gm}

This is the most important idea in the chapter. **"Kill the guard" is an obstacle, and the stake is whatever he's guarding.** A stake is something the crew *wants*, with a cost if they don't get it, and the guard's just in the way of the stake.

| Obstacle | Stake |
| --- | --- |
| Kill the Wardens. | Get the paintings out, or the souls stay in the paint, and Vane moves them somewhere worse. |
| Beat the magistrate. | Get Vesk out tonight, or the Estate's lawyers arrive in the morning, and she hangs. |
| Escape the patrol. | Don't lead them to the safehouse, or it's burned, with everything in it. |
| Find the killer. | Clear Dorrie's name before the funeral, or her family never speaks to her again. |

Give the scene **two or three stakes**, and make sure the crew doesn't have enough actions to do all of them well. That's where the tactics come from. Should the fastest character go for the paintings, or stop the maid? Should the wall hold the corridor, or haul the canvases? Every round they're choosing what they can afford to lose.

Each stake gets a **Challenge** and tags for what's **in the way**: *Locked, Heavy Load, Three of Them, Someone Must Agree, Proof Needed.* Every win strips one, and once they're all stripped, the stake is theirs. Put a **clock** on at least one stake: a die that steps down every round nobody works on it. When it steps down past a d2, the chance is gone.

Say every stake out loud at the start of the scene, with its cost. The choice is only real if they know what they're choosing between.

### A clock on every scene {#s-scene-clock}

Put a rough clock on every scene. It's an event on the whole scene: a die, face up, that steps down at the end of every round, with one sentence for what happens when it runs out. Outside rounds, it steps down whenever the crew wastes time. It tells the players how long they've got, and it tells you where the scene lands if they're too slow.

Start it at a **d6** for a tight scene, a **d8** for most, and a **d10** when there's time. When it steps down past a d2, the scene turns: play what the sentence says, and the scene either carries on in a worse place or ends.

| d8 | The clock | When it runs out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The watch is coming. | They arrive, and everyone still here is caught in the act. |
| 2 | The fire's spreading. | The floor goes. Whoever's left is on the stairs, or on fire. |
| 3 | The ship sails at the tide. | It leaves, with the cargo, the witness, or the way out. |
| 4 | The ritual's nearly done. | It completes. Something wakes, or someone is lost. |
| 5 | The alarm will be raised. | Every door locks, and every threat in the building is coming. |
| 6 | Dawn's coming. | The light finds you, and the night's cover is gone. |
| 7 | The witness is bleeding out. | They die, and what they knew goes with them. |
| 8 | Reinforcements are on the way. | A second wave of threats arrives, fresh, from the worst side. |

### A moment for each character {#s-spotlight}

Before you build, look at every character's sheet and ask what they're the best at. Then put that problem in the scene.

-   Vesk's the best cat burglar in the city, so the vault's three storeys up, behind a lock nobody else can open.
-   Dorrie can talk anyone round. The only witness is a terrified maid who won't listen to anyone in armour.
-   Bram holds lines, and the corridor's two Wardens wide and one Bram wide.
-   Isolde's order made the binding, so she's the only one who can read it, and break it.

This is what makes specialists feel like specialists, so don't make them split their dice across everything. Give each of them a problem that's *theirs*, let them dominate it, and make the scene hard because there are four of those problems at once.

:::example[Building a scene, start to finish]
The crew has to recover a ledger from the Harbourmaster's ball before it's read aloud at midnight. Here's how the GM builds it.

**Where it starts:** The crew's in the ballroom, in borrowed clothes, and the Harbourmaster has just tapped his glass for silence. **In motion:** At midnight he reads the ledger to the whole Guild.

**Stakes:** *Get the ledger* is Challenge 4, with *Locked* and *Watched* in the way. If they fail, every name in it is ruined, including Dorrie's cousin. The second, *Keep your faces out of it*, is Challenge 3, with *An Old Enemy in the Room* (hidden) in the way, and there's a clock on it at d8, while the inspector asks questions. If they fail, the Guild knows exactly who robbed it. The third, *Win the Harbourmaster's wife*, is Challenge 4, with *Loyal* and *Afraid* in the way. If they win, she'll testify against him later. That one's optional, and tempting.

**Who pushes back:** The Harbourmaster (Bestiary), his two guards (mooks), and the old enemy, a Guild inspector who arrested Vesk once and wants to finish the job.

**Ground:** *An Audience*, *Chandelier* and *Balconies*, plus a hidden one: *A Servants' Passage*.

**Moments:** Vesk gets the strongbox in the study, and Dorrie gets the wife. Bram gets the inspector, who won't stay polite. For Isolde, the ledger's warded, and the ward isn't the Guild's work.

**Scene clock:** Midnight, a d10. When it runs out, the Harbourmaster reads the ledger aloud.

**Twist:** The ledger's a fake, and the real one is in the wife's reticule. **Ways out:** through the servants' passage, off a balcony into the harbour, or out the front door as honoured guests.

That's nine sentences, and that scene will run for two hours.
:::

## Building Threats {#s-building-threats}

A threat is a **Challenge** and a handful of **tags**. The full procedure, with tables to roll on, is in [The Threat Builder](#threat-builder). Here's the thinking behind it.

### Challenge is defence {#challenge-is-defence}

Pick the Challenge by what the threat *is*, not by how hard you want the fight to be. The ladder's in [Threats](#s-challenges). If this one's weaker than usual (young, half-woken, hurt), run it a size small, and say why.

### Write tags that change what the players do {#write-tags-that-change-what-the-players-do}

A good tag tells the table how to deal with it, and a bad one's just a number in disguise.

| Bad tag | Good tag | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Strong* | *Hits Like a Wagon* | Tells you not to trade blows. Duck, dodge, or put something big between you. |
| *Hard to Kill* | *Armoured* | Tells you what gets through: a lever, a Piercing bolt, the gap under the arm. |
| *Scary* | *Fear* | Tells you it goes for Confidence, and that naming it takes some of that away. |
| *Smart* | *Reads a Feint* | Tells you the first trick fails. The second, or an honest move, gets through. |
| *Loyal* (to nobody) | *Loyal to Lady Ashe* | Tells you who to talk about, and whose betrayal would break him. |

Every tag is one of three kinds: **how it comes at you**, **what keeps it going**, and **what drives it**. A threat with only the first two is a wall to grind through, so give everything that matters at least one thing that drives it.

### Tags are health {#tags-are-health}

A threat's **keeps-it-going** tags are how many wins it takes to bring it down. It defends and telegraphs with its Challenge die, and tags don't make it bigger unless the tag says so.

| Size | Challenge | Tags | Keeps it going |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Mook** | 2–3 | 2–3, and maybe one hidden | None or one. Falls to one good win. Comes in groups. |
| **Named** | 4–5 | 3–4, and one hidden | Two. A proper fight. Someone with a name and a reason, who can be talked to. |
| **Boss** | 6 | 4 or more, and one hidden | Three or more, some hidden, and a weak point. Tough, if it should last. |

Never count hits. Count the reasons it's still standing. When a player asks "how hurt is it?", tell them which tags are gone.

### Tough {#s-tough-gm}

One win, one tag is right for most threats. A boss that should last needs more than that, so give it [Tough](#s-tough), and think of it as hit points: a number the crew has to knock down, like *Tough 40*. Every winning die takes off what it rolled. Plain *Tough* comes off anything that hurts it. Give it a name, like *Natural Armour 40* or *Commanding Presence 20*, and only what fits it chips it.

-   **Who gets it.** Mostly bosses. A Named threat that should take a while can have a little, and so can a big obstacle: a vault door that's *Reinforced 30*, a crowd that's *Loyal 20*, a storm, a fire.
-   **How much.** Start from your round budget. Half the budget holds the whole crew for about a round. The whole budget holds them for about two. A boss worth a session wants about the whole budget in Tough, split however you like: one big number that comes first, or two smaller ones. The bestiary writes Tough for four characters with one Title each, a budget of 40, so scale it to yours.
-   **Give it things to tackle.** Tough is the hit points. Its other tags are what make the fight: something to knock out of the air, a shield to break, a shout to stop. A boss with Tough and two plain tags is a better fight than one with four numbers, so keep it short.
-   **First, when it's a shell.** Mark Tough *first* when the story says you can't get at the rest until it's gone: a hide, a shield wall, a crowd of bodyguards. Leave it off when the crew should be able to go round it.
-   **Say what each chip looks like:** plates cracking off his shoulders, or his men looking at each other. Then say the number that's left.
-   **Keep the way in open.** A weak point still ends it, an opening counts Tough as just one tag, and Study can find what's under the first one, so a clever crew never has to grind through the whole number.

:::example[Making Redhand last]
Redhand, the boss in [The Threat Builder](#threat-builder), started as three plain tags and a weak point. Against four characters with one Title each, he'd often fall in the first round. Their budget's 40, so he gets about 40 in Tough: *Armoured 20 (first)*, *Relentless* and *Feeds 20*. Now it's about a round of the whole crew to get through his armour, a win for *Relentless*, and another round to stop him feeding. Most crews will spend three or four rounds on him, or one, if they've read Vane's ledger and say his name.
:::

### Hidden tags and weak points {#hidden-tags-and-weak-points}

Anything that matters should have **one hidden tag**, and every boss should have a **weak point**: the tag that holds the whole thing together. Break the binding, speak the true name or show the zealot her own child in the crowd, and it falls, whatever else it's got left.

A weak point is the reward for paying attention: for studying, asking questions, remembering what the old woman in session two said. Make weak points findable, and make finding them feel like winning.

### Powers are tags {#powers-are-tags}

Threats never have Aura. If a threat can do something impossible, write it as a tag (*Calls the Storm, Walks Through Walls, Wears Your Face*) and telegraph it from the budget like anything else. The players see it coming, with a die, and they can answer it. Don't let a power skip the telegraph, because then the players don't get to play against it.

### Unique mechanics {#s-unique-threats}

Most threats need nothing but Challenge and tags. A few deserve a rule of their own, written as a tag. I'd use them sparingly. They're what make a threat memorable.

-   **Crumbling.** Instead of stripping tags, every win steps its Challenge down one, and at Challenge 1 it falls. Use it for crowds, walls, swarms and slowly dying things.
-   **Forms.** It has two or more forms. Strip the last keeps-it-going tag of one and it doesn't fall: it changes. Its Tough refills, and it takes 3 to 5 tags from its Forms list of about ten (more for a bigger change), and the list can bring back tags it had before. Because Tough refills, split the boss's Tough across its forms: Tough 20 each for a boss with two. Its weak point still ends it in any form. Tell the table it's happening, and what it's becoming.
-   **Regrows.** At the end of every round, one stripped tag comes back, unless something the tag names (fire, salt, a word) was used on it this round.
-   **Split.** It's in several places at once, with its keeps-it-going tags spread across its bodies. Strip them in one place and the others carry on.
-   **Layered.** It rolls one Challenge die for every tag it's got left keeping it going, and its best die counts. Strip a tag and it rolls one fewer. Use it for the enchanted door, the warded vault, or the boss that should feel like a wall. The rule's in [Conflict](#s-layered).
-   **Bound by Rules.** It has to obey something, like an invitation, a bargain or a name said three times. Learn the rule and a single roll ends it.

## The Round Budget {#s-budget}

Every round you've got a number of steps to telegraph with. That's your **round budget**, and it's what keeps the pressure matched to the crew.

:::rule
**Round budget: 10 a character with one Title, 15 with three, and 20 with five.** In between, go between: two Titles is about 12 or 13 a character, and four is about 17 or 18. Past five Titles, it's your call.
:::

| Titles each | A character | Four characters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One | 10 | **40** |
| Two | 12–13 | **50** |
| Three | 15 | **60** |
| Four | 17–18 | **70** |
| Five | 20 | **80** |

It's a guide, so budget anywhere in that range and move it to suit the table: if the crew's walking through everything, push harder, and if they're drowning, ease off. If the crew's mixed, add up each character's share. Past the fifth Title the crew's growing in ways the budget doesn't count, like Master tiers and better gear, so go by what you see at the table.

### Spending it {#spending-it}

-   **A telegraph costs its steps.** A d10 is 5, and you take it off the budget as you say it.
-   **Spend it on everything.** Threats, the ground, events, stakes, and anything else you decide the scene needs. A telegraph aimed at a clock or a stake is as good as one aimed at a character, and often better.
-   **Not all of it at bodies.** At Even, aim about one harm telegraph a round at each character, and spend the rest on control, position, stakes, clocks and words. At Hard, about two each.
-   **Answered telegraphs still count.** You spent the pressure, and the crew stopping it is what it was for.
-   **A telegraph at several targets costs its steps once for each target.** *The fire spreads, a d8 at all three on the stair* is 12 steps.
-   **It refills every round.** Unspent steps don't carry over.
-   **Within the budget, the fiction decides.** A single guard doesn't telegraph six times, but a boss with two blades might telegraph three.

### How hard it pushes {#how-hard-it-pushes}

| Setting | Budget | Use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A breather** | Half | Scenes about choices, not survival. Social scenes, a job going well, the first round when the threats are overconfident. |
| **Even** | All of it | Most scenes. The threats push about as hard as the crew can push back. |
| **Hard** | Half again | The fight of the session. The crew can't answer everything, and something gets through. |

:::note
**Keep them stretched.** The crew should never have enough actions to do everything. A ward on a friend, a blessing before the charge, a Study on the boss: they're all good, and every one of them is an action that doesn't go on the stakes. Telegraph enough that those choices hurt.
:::

### How long a scene runs {#s-size-scene}

Add up everything standing between the crew and done: every keeps-it-going tag on every threat that has to fall, and every tag in the way of every stake. Anything with none counts as one. Divide that by half the crew's actions a round, because not every action lands. That's roughly how many rounds the scene will take. Then add about two rounds for every half of your round budget sitting in [Tough](#s-tough), because the crew won't put every action into chipping.

Say it's two Wardens with four keeps-it-going tags each, and two stakes with two tags each. That's 12. Four characters with two actions each is 8 a round, and half of that is 4. So it's about three rounds, or fewer if they find the weak points. If a scene looks like it'll take eight rounds, it'll drag, so I'd cut something or give it an obvious weak point.

## Telegraphing Well {#s-telegraphing}

In a fight, everything the world does comes through a telegraph. Every round you're telling the table what the world's about to do. Do it well and the players will feel the danger before a single die gets rolled.

### Say it as one sentence {#say-it-as-one-sentence}

**Who, does what, to whom, how hard.** *"The captain levels his crossbow at Ash, a d8."* Don't just say *"he attacks."* Every telegraph should be something a player could picture, and something a player could answer.

### Mix what you telegraph {#mix-what-you-telegraph}

A round of five telegraphs that all say "hits someone" is a boring round, so mix them up:

-   **Harm**: the spear at Isolde.
-   **Control**: pin Bram against the wall, grab the child, disarm the duellist.
-   **Position**: cut off the door, plant itself in the vault doorway, push the crew towards the edge.
-   **Stakes**: snatch the painting, burn the ledger, slam the gate.
-   **Clocks**: ring the bell, run for help, pour the oil on the stairs.
-   **Words**: shame Dorrie in front of the court, frighten the witness, turn the crowd.

Let the threat's tags tell you what it does. *Reach* pins from a distance, *Calls for Help* goes after the clock, and *Grasping* goes for what they're carrying. A threat that telegraphs from its tags feels like itself.

### Aim at what they care about {#aim-at-what-they-care-about}

Go for the stake they're winning, the friend with a d2 Armour, the item marked *Wanted* or the bond they wrote in session one. The crew should never feel safe letting a telegraph ride just because it's small.

### Pressure heavy armour {#pressure-heavy-armour}

Armour has no ceiling. A knight in plate with a shield and a helm, guarding, can sit behind a d12 and a second die and shrug off blows all night, and that's what plate's meant to do. Instead of fighting it with bigger numbers, fight it with choices. Aim at what Armour doesn't stop: pin him, drag him, frighten him, or go for the stake behind him, the friend beside him or the clock. Telegraph more than the crew can answer, so the one in plate has to spend his actions standing in front of people instead of swinging. Heavy armour buys time, so make them decide what to spend it on.

### One answer, sparingly {#one-answer-sparingly}

A telegraph with only one answer, like *if this lands, the house wakes*, is a spotlight. Use one or two a scene, on things that really matter, and always say so when you telegraph it.

### The quick telegraph {#the-quick-telegraph}

You don't need to prepare every telegraph. When the scene needs something (the candles gutter, the floor groans, a dog starts barking two streets away), just put a die on the table and say what it is. *"The floorboards under the gallery are giving way. A d8, at anyone standing there."* It comes off the budget like anything else.

### Telegraph at their strengths {#telegraph-at-their-strengths}

Give the wall something big to answer, the eye something hidden to find, and the voice someone to talk to. Telegraphs are how you threaten the crew, and they're also how you invite each of them to be brilliant.

## Running a Round {#s-running-round}

1.  **Telegraph.** Everything goes face up and comes off the budget. Say which telegraphs have only one answer.
2.  **Ask what they do.** They go in whatever order they like. Let them talk and plan, but keep it moving.
3.  **Let them roll for your side.** When a player acts against a threat, they roll its Challenge die too, in another colour, once for each intent aimed at it. A *Layered* threat rolls one die for every tag it's got left keeping it going. You'll hardly touch a die all round.
4.  **Say what the win does.** The player's intent happens: strip the tag, place the condition, move the threat, reveal the secret. If they made an opening, a Mook or a Named threat falls, and a Boss loses two tags, unless it was its weak point. On Tough, take off what the die rolled and say what's left.
5.  **End the round.** Everything unanswered lands, and the players roll Armour or Confidence against each die aimed at them, and the telegraph's die as well. For each one that lands, name what it costs, and take a fair swap if they offer one. Untouched events and clocks step down, and every condition steps down one size. Spend the ones you said you would.
6.  **Describe the new situation.** Then telegraph again.

### Judging intents {#judging-intents}

Players will give their dice all kinds of intents. These three questions settle almost everything:

-   **Is it possible from here, with this?** If not, it's "not like that," and you tell them what would work.
-   **Is it one intent or two?** "Knock him down and take his sword" is two dice, or one die and a choice.
-   **What does it get past?** If the intent goes around everything that's keeping a threat going, say so. That's an opening, and they've earned it.

### Recognise openings {#recognise-openings}

When a player shoves the shielded captain off a narrow bridge, *Shield* and *Fortified* do nothing for him. When the thief puts a knife to the throat of a guard who never saw her, his *Trained* doesn't help. Say it out loud: *"That's an opening. If you win, he's done."* (Against a Boss: *"that's two tags."*) If players get rewarded for clever play, they'll keep playing cleverly.

## Consequences and Failure {#s-gm-consequences}

**A miss only costs something when it matters.** When something pushes back or time's short, a miss lands a consequence. When nothing's at stake, a miss is just a miss: the lock didn't open, so try something else.

**Name the cost.** Pick one that fits the blow and the threat: Armour, Confidence, a Hurt, an item, their Aspiration, the party Goal or a clock. A threat's tags are a good guide: *Venom* poisons, and *Grasping* takes what they carry. If the player offers a different one that fits and costs about as much, take it. If the telegraph had one answer, it happens.

**Fail forward.** When a miss matters outside a round, change the situation. The lock holds and the guard's footsteps are on the stair, or the magistrate refuses and now he knows your name. The Twist table in [The Oracle](#oracle) is full of these.

**Make a Blaze magnificent.** A player who fills their last Hurt box gets to declare something extraordinary, with no roll. Let it be as big as the moment, then give it room: the scene afterwards should feel different because of it.

## Secrets and Discovery {#s-discovery}

-   **Put a secret in everything that matters.** Every named threat, every stake and every important place gets one hidden tag.
-   **Reveal generously.** When a player looks at something nobody's hiding, with a permission that plainly covers it, tell them without a roll. A threat's hidden tags take Study, because the threat pushes back.
-   **When they Study and win, give them what they asked for.** That's a hidden tag, a straight answer, or something here that helps. If what they're looking for could be here, it's here.
-   **Leave three trails to every important secret.** A person who knows, a place that shows it and a thing that proves it. If they miss one, they'll find another.
-   **Let secrets change the fight.** The best hidden tags turn a slog into a single roll: the weak point, the want, the name.

## Area, Spark and Study at the Table {#s-gm-area}

-   **Big things are big Challenges.** Through the vault door, across the city: set the Challenge by how hard it is in the world, with tags for what stands in the way, and let them roll.
-   **Say what an area touches, before they roll.** Fire catches everyone in the squares it covers, friend and foe. If a friend's standing in the fireball, say so, and let them make it smaller. A ward or a rallying shout counts friends instead, and only ones who can hear it, see the signal or stand in the circle.
-   **Hand out Sparks.** When a player does something that makes the story better and their Spark's spent, give them one. It's your best way of saying *more of that*. The other way they get one is [trouble](#s-trouble-gm).
-   **Say yes to Sparks.** If one's too big, offer the smaller version. It has to be something their character would have done.
-   **Study finds what's there.** If it could be in the scene, it's there. A Spark can make something true that wasn't, but Study can't.

## Quick Rulings {#s-rulings}

When the rules don't say, find the nearest rule. Most questions come down to one of these.

| The situation | The ruling |
| --- | --- |
| Nothing on the sheet fits. | The action alone is one step: a d2. If something almost fits, it's a stretch: 1 Aura. |
| How hard is it? Nothing's fighting back. | A Challenge. 2: easy for a pro. 3: it takes care. 4: hard. 5: very hard. 6: the best in the world might fail. A tag for each thing that makes it worse. |
| The whole crew does the same thing. | One leads and rolls. Anyone who can help, Helps. Anyone who's a liability (loud armour, nothing that fits) costs the leader's roll a step. |
| Two things in one action. | Split the die: one extra die for each action spent. Or spend a second action. |
| The crew strikes first. | If the crew acts before the threats know, the GM telegraphs nothing that round. Anyone who never saw it coming is helpless. |
| An ally fights beside the crew. | It's a threat on your side: a Challenge and tags. A player spends an action to direct it: roll its Challenge die. Threats can telegraph at it. It has no Hurt boxes. Strip its tags. |
| Fire, falls, floods, traps. | The world telegraphs: a die at whoever's in it, from the budget. Answer it or let it ride. |
| Something that lasts a while: a poison, a spell, an alarm. | A condition, if it sits on someone. A clock or an event, if it sits on the scene. |
| A long task: research for a day, forging papers. | Outside rounds, one action or two, as the GM says. In downtime, a slot. |
| Something big and strange the rules don't cover. | Is it an intent (roll against a Challenge), a stretch (1 Aura), or a Spark? It's almost always one of those. |
| They want to know something about the world. | If a permission plainly covers knowing it, tell them. If nobody knows, roll the [Oracle](#oracle). |
| A player is taken prisoner, or knocked out. | Only a one-answer telegraph, a Blaze, or the player's choice does that. Say so when you telegraph it. |
| A resting break between scenes. | Aura refills when the next scene starts. Hurt waits for downtime. |
| A player argues a permission fits. | It's close: 1 Aura, and move on. |

## Pacing {#s-pacing}

-   **Start late, leave early.** Open scenes in the middle of things, and close them the moment the stakes are settled.
-   **Rounds only when timing matters.** A fight the crew's obviously won doesn't need three more rounds, so narrate the end and move on.
-   **Two or three scenes a session.** Something like a planning scene, a confrontation and an aftermath. There's more in [The Session](#the-session).
-   **Cut to the good part.** The walk to the manor is one sentence, unless something's waiting on the way.
-   **Downtime every session.** It keeps the world moving and lets the players choose how they grow. Keep it brisk: pressures, slots, and one roll if something pushes back.
-   **Watch the table, not the notes.** When the energy drops, play the twist early, and when a scene's singing, let it run.

## Campaigns {#s-campaigns}

-   **An arc is four to six sessions.** It's got a party Goal, a faction moving against the crew, and at least one Aspiration coming to a head.
-   **Aspirations are your map.** Every player's written down what their character wants, so build scenes that put it within reach, then make them choose between it and something else.
-   **Titles are the milestones.** When an Aspiration resolves and a Title gets written, the campaign's turned a page, so mark it.
-   **The world grows with them.** The budget rises with every Title, and threats that were bosses become lieutenants. Factions notice the crew and start treating it as a power (see [Factions](#factions)).
-   **Remember everything.** Bring back the maid who swore she saw a stranger, and the Warden who knelt, because a world that remembers is one worth being competent in.

## The GM on One Page {#s-gm-checklist}

:::rule
**Before the session.** Two or three scenes, each with stakes, a scene clock, a twist and a moment for everyone, plus one secret in everything that matters.

**At the table.** Describe, ask, say yes, and only roll when something pushes back. Be generous with permissions and consistent with rulings. When they look at you, make a move, and bring each player trouble about once a session.

**In a round.** Telegraph everything, face up, within the budget: 10 a character with one Title, 15 with three and 20 with five, and in between for two and four. Mix harm, control, position, stakes, clocks and words, about one harm telegraph each at Even. Let the players roll the threats' dice, let wins change things, and recognise openings.

**Consequences.** Only when it matters. Name the cost, take a fair swap, and fail forward.

**Area, Spark, Study.** Say what an area touches. Hand out Sparks for great moments, and say yes to them. If what they Study for could be there, it's there.

**Conditions.** Face up, with their die, and say when you'll spend them.

**After.** Downtime every session, and every faction takes its turn. Let the world move, and remember everything.
:::
